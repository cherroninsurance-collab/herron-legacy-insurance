**Caption:**
This one costs people real money, and it's 100% avoidable.

There are two ways to move retirement money, and they are not the same thing.

**Direct (trustee-to-trustee):**
▪ Money goes custodian to custodian
▪ No 60-day clock
▪ No mandatory withholding
▪ Generally not reportable as a distribution
▪ No once-per-year limit

**Indirect (they hand you the money):**
▪ 60 days to get it fully redeposited
▪ From an employer plan like a 401(k), 20% is withheld — and you have to replace that 20% out of
pocket or it's treated as a taxable distribution
▪ IRA-to-IRA indirect rollovers are limited to one per 12 months across all your IRAs
▪ Miss it and you can owe income tax, plus a 10% penalty if you're under 59½

Then the second trap, the one nobody warns you about — the cost of *leaving*:

▪ Surrender charges on your current annuity or CD
▪ A market value adjustment
▪ Bonus recapture if you took a premium bonus
▪ Losing a rate you'll never get back

Sometimes the right answer is "wait fourteen months." I've told people that. It costs me a sale and
saves them four figures.

## POST 1 — The "safe" money that isn't

Your money in the bank is losing a slow, quiet fight and the statement will never tell you.

I'm not anti-bank. I have a checking account like everybody else.

But let's be honest about what a savings account is actually for.

It's for emergencies. Six months of expenses. Cash you can get at on a Tuesday afternoon.

It was never designed to be a retirement plan.

Here's what happens when it becomes one:

▪ You earn a rate the bank can change whenever it wants
▪ Inflation quietly takes a bite every single year
▪ You get a 1099 for the privilege
▪ And it does nothing to create income later

Meanwhile the guy down the street has half his savings doing the exact same job — sitting still —
except his version has a contractual floor and a lifetime income switch on it.

Same "safe." Different outcome at 67.

I'm not saying pull your emergency fund. Keep it. Please keep it.

I'm saying: look at the pile *above* your emergency fund and ask what job it's actually doing.

If the answer is "nothing, it's just sitting there because I don't want to lose it" — that's fine.
But there are places that also don't lose to the index and do something.

So, honest question — how much of your savings is genuinely for emergencies, and how much is just
sitting there because the market scared you at some point?

## POST 2 — For the owner-operators

You built a business that pays you exactly nothing the day you stop driving.

That's not an insult. That's just the deal nobody wrote down for you.

W-2 guys get a 401(k) match and somebody else's HR department nagging them about it.

You get a truck payment, a fuel card, quarterly estimates, and complete silence about retirement.

Here's the trap I see over and over with owner-operators:

▪ Great years where the money feels endless
▪ Rough years where you dip into everything you saved
▪ No consistent contribution, because income isn't consistent
▪ Equity locked in a truck that depreciates while you sleep
▪ And a retirement plan that's really just "sell the truck"

The truck is not a retirement plan. It's a depreciating asset with a warranty.

What actually works for irregular income is a system built *for* irregular income:

▪ Fund it lump-sum in the good years, not monthly
▪ Zero floor, so a bad market year doesn't stack on a bad freight year
▪ A switch you can flip later that pays you monthly for as long as you're breathing

That last part matters more for you than almost anybody, because nobody is sending you a pension.
There is no company. There's you.

I grew up around people who worked with their hands and got told to "just invest." Nobody ever
showed them how.

So here's my question for the drivers: what's your actual plan for the first month you don't get in
the truck? Not the dream — the plan.

## POST 3 — The 12% lie

If your advisor is still selling you on a "12% average return," ask him what he averaged in 2008.

I'll wait.

Here's why averages are the most dangerous number in finance.

Year one: +100%. Year two: −50%.

Average return: +25%.

Actual account: exactly where you started.

That's not a trick I made up. That's arithmetic, and it happens in real portfolios every cycle.

Now stack the part nobody mentions — you're withdrawing money while it's down.

▪ Down 20% → you need +25% just to get back to even
▪ Down 40% → you need +67%
▪ Down 50% → you need +100%
▪ And every dollar you pulled out in the hole never comes back to help

At 30, none of this matters. You've got 35 years and a paycheck. Ride it out.

At 55? You have maybe ten more years of contributions and then you start taking money *out*.
The order your returns show up in matters more than the average.

I'm not telling you to sell your 401(k). I'd never say that.

I'm telling you that "average" is a rearview-mirror number, and you're driving forward.

So what's the number you were told to expect — and did anybody ever show you what happens if the
bad years land in the wrong order?

## POST 4 — Your own pension

Your grandfather retired at 62 with a check that showed up every month until he died. You got a
401(k) and a hope.

Pensions didn't vanish because they stopped working.

They vanished because carrying that promise on a corporate balance sheet got expensive, and
companies figured out they could hand the risk to you instead.

The promise still exists. It just has to be bought individually now.

Strip a pension down and it's only three parts:

▪ Money goes in
▪ It sits for some years
▪ A check comes out every month, for life

That's it. That's the whole machine.

You can build that yourself. You fund it, you pick the date you switch it on, and the carrier is
contractually on the hook to pay you — at 85, at 92, at 100.

The catch, said plainly because you deserve to hear it:

▪ That money is less liquid than a brokerage account
▪ The guarantee is only as strong as the carrier behind it, so ratings matter
▪ It's not FDIC insured — it's an insurance contract, not a bank product
▪ And you give up some upside to get the certainty

Those are real trade-offs. Anyone who tells you there's no downside is selling, not advising.

But here's the thing I keep coming back to: not one person I've ever sat with regretted having a
check they couldn't outlive. The regret always runs the other direction.

Question for the room — if you could turn part of your savings into a paycheck for life, what
number would it have to hit before you'd actually do it?

## POST 5 — Where I agree with the anti-annuity crowd

Half the guys yelling "annuities are terrible" on the internet are right — about the products they
happen to be describing.

I sell these things and I'll still say it.

Some annuities *are* bad deals:

▪ Variable annuities with stacked M&E charges, sub-account fees, and a rider on top
▪ 14-year surrender schedules sold to a 78-year-old
▪ Anything sold on a "guaranteed 7% roll-up" without explaining that the roll-up applies to a
benefit base you can't withdraw
▪ Any contract where the agent won't put the cap and the surrender schedule in writing

That's not a rare abuse. That's a real thing that happens, and it's why the whole category has the
reputation it has.

Here's where the internet gets lazy though: "annuity" is a category, not a product. A MYGA and a
variable annuity have about as much in common as a bicycle and a semi.

So instead of picking a team, ask the three questions that actually expose a bad deal:

▪ What's my cap, participation rate, or spread — and can you change it after year one?
▪ What does every rider cost me per year, in dollars?
▪ What's the surrender schedule, and what can I withdraw each year without a charge?

Write the answers down. Make them sign it.

If the answers are clean and the product still fits your gap, it's a fine tool. If the agent gets
squirmy, you just saved yourself a decade.

Real question: has anyone actually gotten straight answers to those three the first time they
asked? I want to know how common that is.

## POST 6 — The question everybody's afraid to ask

"What happens to the money if I die early?" — ask it out loud. Any agent who flinches at that
question just told you everything.

It's the number one thing people are scared of and the number one thing they don't say in the
appointment.

The old fear comes from a real thing: with a straight life annuitization, you could hand over a
lump sum, get two checks, pass away, and the insurance company keeps the rest.

That structure still exists. And for most families, it's the wrong choice.

What most people actually use now looks different:

▪ The contract keeps a cash value with a named beneficiary
▪ Whatever's left generally passes to that beneficiary
▪ It passes by beneficiary designation, so it usually skips probate
▪ You can add period-certain or joint options so payments continue to a spouse

Every one of those has a cost or a trade-off. Joint income means smaller checks. Period-certain
means smaller checks. Nothing is free — but you get to *choose* the structure.

The mistake isn't picking the wrong option. It's not knowing there were options.

Ask it directly, in these words: "If I die in year three, what does my spouse get, and what does
the company keep?"

Then watch the face.

So let me ask the room — has an agent, advisor, or banker ever volunteered that answer to you
before you had to drag it out of them?
